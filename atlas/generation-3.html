The Chronicle

01-stone

The first day: stone

In the beginning the world was all abyss, a floor with nothing standing on it. Stone woke there, and did not hurry.

Stone raised a land in the west first. It reaches from its north shore at (7,2) to a narrow cape at (16,29). Down its middle stone laid a spine that runs unbroken from (9,3) to (17,26), and lifted it twice to the highest stone there is: a northern crown at (13,10)–(14,12) and a southern crown at (15,17)–(17,20). Stone set the spine where the land lies farthest from every shore. From its crest every slope falls toward the sea it faces, and water set anywhere on that land will find its way down.

West of the spine the ground stays high and then ends. Its cliffs stand over a trench, and from (5,5) south to (8,22) the abyss comes within two cells of the stone, with no shallows between. Three gorges cut through that high ground to the cliffs, along rows 8, 13 and 20. The middle one reaches the sea at (6,13), the lowest ground on all that shore.

East of the spine the land steps down to a broad plain beside a narrow sea. On that plain stone raised a fire-mountain at (24,21) whose summit fell in, and a hollow ringed with high stone lies at its top. Northeast of the spine's head stone pressed the ground down into a basin at (18,7)–(21,10), rimmed on every side. Whatever flows into either place stays there.

Across the narrow sea stone raised a second land, older and lower, from (36,7) in the north to two horns in the south at (35,24) and (39,24). One worn range runs down its middle and crests at (37,10). At the range's southern end stone broke the land. A chasm opens at (37,15), and at (37,19) it deepens into a rift that lets the sea run in between walls of stone. The rift reaches the southern sea past (35,25).

Between the two lands, at (30,18), stone left an isle on a shallow bank that runs from shore to shore along row 18.

Then stone threw stone into the deep. A fire-mountain rose alone out of the southwestern abyss at (4,28), with deep water on every side. Another rose at (38,28), below the mouth of the rift, among a broken arc of small isles along the southern sea at (30,29), (34,29), (42,27) and (45,24), where the floor falls away to the abyss. A single rock stands in the northern mouth of the narrow sea at (30,2). Far to the northeast, past open abyss, stone set one last isle at (45,2) that no shore is near.

Last, stone considered the sea, which had not yet come. Stone could not choose how high it would rise, and did not try. Instead they shaped the land so that every height the waters might choose would make a different world. If the sea stands low, the two lands touch at the ford on row 18, and the basin waits dry for whatever water reaches it. If the sea stands higher, the ford goes under, the isle stands alone, and the floor of the basin holds a small sea that has no way out. Higher still, the eastern plain drowns and only the high country is left.

Then stone's day ended, and all of it was fixed.

02-waters

The second day: waters

On the second day the god of waters woke over a world of bare stone standing in the abyss. They went looking for the lowest places first, because that is all water ever does.

They did not hurry the sea. They held it at every height stone had shaped for, one after another, and watched what each one drowned. Then they let it rise to 3 and no further. Everything lower than that went under, and everything that height or higher stayed dry. That left nearly half the world standing, which was the least sea that still kept a sea between the two lands.

Where the sea stopped, the shores were drawn. Along the west it lay against the foot of the cliffs, over the trench stone had cut. In the north it made a long, even shore from (7,2) to (19,3). Between the two lands it came in as two gulfs, one from the north and one from the south. On row 18 it stopped. There the bank stone had left lay at exactly the sea's height and stayed dry, so the two lands touched. The neck of ground between them runs from (26,18) to (28,18). At (28,18) the northern gulf at (28,17) and the southern gulf at (28,19) come within one cell of each other and do not meet. Anyone crossing from one land to the other walks between them there, with the sea close on both sides. Nothing that floats passes from one gulf to the other.

Offshore, the rock in the northern mouth at (30,2) stood up as a small islet. The far isle at (45,2) stood alone in the northeastern deep. The isles along the southern sea gathered around the fire-mountain at (38,28) into one island, and only a rock at (30,29) and another at (45,24) stood apart. The fire-mountain at (4,28) stood alone with deep water all around it. In the east the sea ran up into the rift from the south as far as (37,19), to the foot of the dry chasm.

Then the god of waters set springs in the high places.

The two crowns of the western spine became the sources of the world's rivers. The northern crown gave three. One runs north from (13,10) to the shore at (13,2). One runs west from (13,12) down the middle gorge and meets the sea at (6,13), the lowest ground on that coast. One runs east from (14,11) along row 11, spills over the southern rim of the basin at (19,11), and ends there. The southern crown gave four. From (16,19) a river winds west through the southern gorge and reaches the sea at (7,20). It is thirteen cells long, the longest river in the world. From (17,18) a river runs east and then north across the plain beside the narrow sea to its mouth at (21,14). From (16,22) a river runs south down the land's long tail to the cape at (15,29). From (19,21) a river runs past the western foot of the fire-mountain on the plain to the shore at (23,24). On the high saddle between the crowns, at (15,15), one more spring rises. Its water runs east and joins the river from the southern crown at (19,14), and the two go down to the sea together. It is the only meeting of rivers anywhere in the world. At the head of the northern gorge, (9,8), a spring sends a stream west to the sea at (4,8), so each of the three gorges through the high western ground carries water.

The god of waters made lakes only where stone had already shaped a hollow. The floor of the basin, from (19,8) to (20,9), filled with still water. The river from the northern crown runs into it, and nothing runs out. A dry ring of ground lies around it, like a shore. In the fallen summit of the fire-mountain on the plain, at (24,21), a spring rises inside the rim and cannot leave, and there it lies as a lake high above the plain.

On the eastern land, the old crest sends one stream north from (37,10) to (37,7) and one west from (37,11) to (32,11). Another runs east from (38,14) to (41,14). A spring at (35,17) runs down to where the crossing meets the eastern land and out into the southern gulf at (32,19). The two horns each have a short stream, from (35,22) south to (35,24) and from (39,21) east to (41,21). Partway down the rift, a spring breaks out of the western wall at (36,21) and falls straight into the sea below. No water was sent into the chasm between (37,15) and (37,18). It is dry, as stone made it.

On the isle at the middle of the crossing, (30,18), a spring rises and runs north into the northern gulf. It is the only fresh water in the middle of the crossing. Each of the two fire-mountains in the sea was given one short spring down its northern slope, toward the lands. The far isle and the rock in the northern mouth were given none.

Some ground was left far from any fresh water, on purpose. The northwest shoulder of the western land around (6,3) is one such place. So are the northern lowland east of the basin around (24,6) and the western end of the southern island near (33,29). The god of waters wanted no one to wake far from water. They also knew that water everywhere would make every place the same.

Then their day ended, and the sea, the springs and the lakes were fixed.

03-sky

The third day: sky

On the third day the god of sky woke over stone and still water. Nothing yet moved over them. The sky had waited for this. Weather over no land is only noise, and now there was land.

First they laid down warmth. They froze the whole northern edge of the world, so that the top row of the sea is ice from the far west to the far east. From there the world grows warmer toward the south. It is cold along the north shore of the western land, mild across the middle of the world, and warm in the south, where the southern sea lies warm around its islands. They made every height colder than the ground below it. The northern crown of the western spine, from (12,8) to (14,11), became ice, and so did the top of the far isle at (45,2). The southern crown was left cold above a warm plain. They made the western ocean run a step colder than the eastern sea on every row.

Then they sent two winds.

North of the middle of the world, the wind comes out of the west, off the cold ocean. It carries little, and less the farther north it blows. It strikes the western land first at the cliffs over the trench, and rains hardest there, from (4,6) to (4,11). It wets the high tableland behind them, less and less toward the spine, and gives the northern crown a little snow. Then it crosses the crest and has nothing left. Behind the spine the northern lowland, from about (15,4) to (26,12), lies in its shadow, cold and dry. The basin and its lake lie in that dryness. The lake is fed by its river and by nothing that falls. Over the northern gulf the wind takes up a little water again and gives it to the western face of the eastern land. The eastern side of that land's crest, around (40,9), is dry.

South of the middle, the wind comes out of the east, off the warm sea. It carries much more. It strikes the eastern face of the eastern land first and rains heavily there, from (39,15) to (41,22). It climbs the worn crest and comes down the other side spent and warmed. The ground from (32,16) to (34,22), facing the southern gulf, is dry and warm. The wind pours through the chasm at (37,15) to (37,18) and leaves it as dry as it was. In the rift below, it takes up water from the sea between the walls and drops it on the walls, so the rift's walls drip where the rift spring breaks out at (36,21).

The southern gulf is narrow, and the east wind reaches the western land only half refilled. The fire-mountain on the plain takes most of what it carries. Its eastern flank, from (25,20) to (26,23), is wet. Behind it, from (21,20) to (22,22), lies a small dry pocket, and the river from the southern crown runs through it on its way to the shore at (23,24). South of the fire-mountain there is nothing in the wind's way but open sea. There it waters the south shore of the plain and the eastern side of the land's long tail down to the cape at (15,29). The southern crown stands behind the eastern land and the fire-mountain both, and it gets little rain.

Then the east wind comes down the western side of the southern crown with nothing left in it, and it heats as it falls. The whole tableland west of that crown and south of row 16 is dry. Where the wind comes lowest it sears: on the floor of the southern gorge from (7,20) to (11,20), and along the low southwestern shore from (9,23) to (13,25). Nothing falls there at all, and nowhere in the world is hotter. The longest river in the world crosses that country to the sea at (7,20). Just offshore the western ocean lies cold.

The two winds meet along a line from about (0,16) at the western edge to (47,12) at the eastern. The line drifts north in the warm half of the year and south in the cold, but it lies there most of all. Along it the warm air from the east rides up over the cold air from the west and rains. At sea that makes a band of wetter weather across the whole world. Where the line lies across high ground it drowns the ground. It does so in two places. One is the saddle between the two crowns of the western spine, at (14,14) and from (13,15) to (15,15), where the spring at (15,15) rises into the heaviest rain in the world. The other is the old crest of the eastern land, at (37,12), (37,13), (38,13) and (38,14), among the springs of that land's rivers. The god of sky had meant to make only one drowning country. They got two from the same cause, and left both.

South of the line, the wet belt ends against the desert. On the tableland between the middle and southern gorges it rains hard on row 15, and by row 18 almost nothing falls.

Out at sea the islands took the winds as the lands did. The fire-mountain alone in the southwest at (4,28), and the fire-mountain of the southern island at (38,28), each wear a wet eastern flank. Behind each lies dry ground. The western end of the southern island near (33,29) is one such dry place. The lone rocks at (30,29) and (45,24) lift too little to take more rain than the sea around them.

The god of sky did not rain to mend the work of anyone before them. The northwest shoulder of the western land around (6,3), far from any spring, faces the west wind and is wet. The northern lowland around (24,6) is in the shadow and stays thirsty. They left the lakes to lie under whatever sky the wind gave them. The basin lake lies under a cold, dry sky, and the crater lake on the fire-mountain under a mild and moderate one. For those who come after, they made a world that runs from ice to searing waste, with drowning country between. Only some of its places are kind.

Then their day ended, and the warmth and the rain were fixed.

04-blight

The fourth day: blight

On the fourth day the god of blight woke over stone, water and weather, and went looking for the places where things could not leave. They did not strike anything. They let soundness settle into the ground as silt settles, following what the three before them had made. Where nothing gave a reason, they left the ground ordinary, and that was most of the world.

They went first to the crown of the western spine, from (12,8) to (14,11). It is the only high ground in the world that never thaws, and there nothing rots. They hallowed it as far as anything can be hallowed. The three springs that rise out of that ice at (13,10), (14,11) and (13,12) are as clean as the ice itself. The rivers they send out carry that cleanness down and lose it slowly. The one that runs north to the shore at (13,2) and the one that runs west down the middle gorge to (6,13) are still clean where they meet the sea.

The third river runs east along row 11 into the basin, and this was the work the god of blight had come for. The river leaves the crown as the purest water in the world. At (19,11) it goes over the basin's rim, at (19,10) it crosses the shore, and it ends in the lake from (19,8) to (20,9). Nothing leaves that lake. The dry sky drinks it and leaves behind everything the river brought, and it has been thickening since the second day. The god of blight made it the foulest place in the world. Its shore became a crust of salt, and its rim sickened. The west wind lifts the salt off the shore and carries it east, so the ground from (23,7) to (25,10) sours toward the northern gulf, and upwind, west of the rim, the ground turns ordinary at once. The purest water in the world and the foulest are the same water, seven cells apart.

Then they went to the fire-mountain on the plain. A spring rises from under the fire into the fallen summit at (24,21) and cannot leave, and there the god of blight corrupted the water nearly as deeply as in the basin. Only the spring's own rising keeps it from being wholly still. The east wind takes the crater's breath west over the rim and lays it in the dry pocket behind the mountain, from (21,20) to (22,22), where no rain falls to wash it out. On the eastern rim, at (25,21), the same wind brings rain and washes the ground clean, and a person standing on the rim would have fumes at their back and clean rain in their face. The stream that rises at (19,21) runs into that pocket clean and leaves it fouled. It carries the mountain's breath to the shore at (23,24).

Over the sea the god of blight did little, because the sea moves and carries off what it takes. They made the frozen northern edge clean, since nothing rots in ice. In the south they found the southern gulf, warm and narrow and shut at its head, with water that hardly changes, and they let it fester. It is worst at its head at (28,19). Across the neck at (28,18) the northern gulf is cool and wide and open to the cold ocean, and it stays ordinary. The two waters come within a cell of each other and are not alike. In the rift the sea runs in between the walls and stops. Above the spring at (36,21) the water lies dead, at (37,19) and (37,20). Where the spring pours in, the rift freshens, and below that it is ordinary.

Every spring in the world they made clean at its mouth. The ones on cold heights or in drowning rain they made cleaner: the southern crown's springs at (17,18) and (16,19), the saddle spring at (15,15), and the springs of the old eastern crest at (37,10), (37,11) and (38,14). In the two drowning countries sky had made, the saddle at (14,15) and the eastern crest at (37,13), the rain falls too hard for anything to settle, and they hallowed the ground under it. Where rain falls heavily on a slope, it scours the slope clean. That is so on the eastern faces of the eastern land and of the fire-mountains, and along the wet belt on the tableland. They left every river clean except the two that run into still water or through the crater's breath.

Where the land lies barely above the sea, flat and warm and wet, the god of blight let a low fever settle. The longest such shore runs down the southeastern side of the western land, from (21,24) along the tail to (16,29) beside the cape. A shorter one lies on the eastern shore of the eastern horns, from (41,22) to (39,24), and a scrap on the plain's shore by the southern gulf. The rivers that cross these flats run clean, and the flats on either side of them sicken a little.

They left the rest alone. The searing country stayed ordinary. Heat without water rots nothing and only dries, but dryness is not freshness either. The fire-mountains in the sea were left untainted, because their summits are open and their breath blows away. The thirsty places waters and sky had left alone, the northwest shoulder and the lowland around (24,6), stayed as they were, except where the basin's salt blows over them. The far isle at (45,2) was the last place they went. Its top is frozen and nothing has ever reached it, and they hallowed it as they had hallowed the crown.

When they were done, more of the world was clean than corrupted. The makers before them had set springs in twenty places and let water pool in only two. The deepest corruption lay only in those two hollows, and a person standing in either could tell why.

Then their day ended, and the soundness of the land was fixed.

05-growth

The fifth day: growth

On the fifth day the god of growth woke over a world that was made in every way but one. Nothing lived on it. They did not hurry to cover it. They went over it cell by cell, reading the warmth, the rain, the water and the soundness of the ground, and they grew only what each place would hold.

They began in the cold. Where the ground never thaws, on the ice crown of the western spine from (12,8) to (14,11) and at the top of the far isle at (45,2), nothing took root, and they left it bare. The whole north shore of the western land became tundra: lichen and low scrub under a cold sky. Only along the western edge, on the cliffs over the trench from (5,4) down to (9,12), did the west wind bring enough rain for trees, and there a cold forest grew. Inland it thinned to meadow, then to tundra again toward the spine. Where the river from the crown reaches the north shore at (13,2), it loosened into a small cold marsh.

Behind the spine the lowland stayed what sky had made it, cold and dry, and grew only scrub. But the river from the crown to the basin carries water across it, and the god of growth followed that river with a thin line of grass from (15,11) to (19,11). At the basin's shore, at (19,10), the grass gave way to scrub. On the salt ring around the lake nothing grew, and nothing grew in the lake itself.

Along the wet belt, from the western cliffs to the saddle between the crowns, the tableland became forest. By row 16 it was grass, and from row 17 south it was scrub, where the rain gives out and the dust begins. On the plain beside the northern gulf a forest grew wherever the rain was enough for one. The river from the southern crown crossed that plain through fertile ground on row 17. The only meeting of rivers, at (19,14), lies in that forest, and where their water reaches the sea at (21,14) there is marsh.

Where sky had drowned the high ground, the god of growth found no trees could stand. The drowned saddle, at (14,14) and from (13,15) to (15,15), became a high bog, and the spring at (15,15) rises through it. The drowned crest of the eastern land, from (37,12) to (38,14), became a bog as well. Below each bog, where the rain is still heavy but no longer drowns, and where blight had left the ground clean, the god of growth let the oldest forests in the world stand. The larger rings the drowned crest of the eastern land, the older land, in eleven cells from (35,11) around to (40,16), with the crest's clean springs running out through it. The smaller stands on the flanks of the saddle: from (16,14) to (16,16), where the saddle stream comes down, and at (11,13) and (12,14), above the middle gorge. There are eighteen cells of such forest in all.

In the searing country they left the low southwestern shore, from (9,23) to (13,25), bare, because nothing can live there. The walls of the southern gorge grew only desert scrub. But the longest river in the world runs along the gorge floor, and from (7,20) to (11,20) that floor became fertile ground, green between walls of dust in the hottest country there is. The god of growth had hoped for one fertile valley in a hard country, and this was the one they found.

South of the fire-mountain on the plain, the tail of the western land came out dry on its western side and wet on its eastern. Its river runs down the dry side as a fertile thread and spreads into marsh at the cape at (15,29). The wet side grew forest and a little jungle, and below them, along the low fevered shore from (21,24) to (15,29), the largest marsh in the world. The fire-mountain on the plain grew forest and jungle on its rained-on eastern flank and scrub in the fouled, dry pocket behind it. Nothing grew on its western rim.

The neck between the two lands at (26,18) to (28,18) stayed dry, with grass at its western end and scrub where the two gulfs almost meet. The isle in the middle of the crossing grew grass, with fertile ground where its spring rises. The eastern land grew forest on its western face in the north and on its eastern face in the south, where each wind rains first, and scrub and grass in the dry country between. A fertile thread runs along the stream from (35,17) to the southern gulf. The chasm stayed dry scrub. The fevered flats on the horns became marsh.

The two fire-mountains in the sea take the east wind warm and wet on their eastern flanks. There the god of growth grew most of the world's jungle, around (5,28) and around (39,28), and on the isle at (42,27). Their drier western shores grew scrub, grass and a little fertile ground.

When they were done, more than a third of the land grew only scrub, tundra or desert life. They did not paint the world green. Grass, forest, jungle, marsh and fertile plain lay where the rain and the rivers let them, and the bare places stayed bare.

Then, on the last day, the god of growth breathed into the first peoples. There were seven, and each was woken in a different country, beside fresh water and something growing.

At (6,9), in the cold forest on the cliffs over the trench, by the stream from the northern gorge, they breathed hardiness in cold and wet.

At (17,10), in the cold lowland, on the clean river two cells from the salt lake, they breathed a tongue that tells clean water from foul.

At (12,20), at the bend where the longest river turns west into the searing gorge, they breathed endurance of heat.

At (20,13), on the dry bank by the only meeting of rivers, with forest across the water, they breathed a care for growing things.

At (31,18), on the isle in the middle of the crossing, between its spring and the stream from the eastern land, they breathed the wish to go between.

At (39,15), in the ancient forest under the drowned eastern crest, they breathed long memory.

At (5,27), on the fire-mountain that stands alone in the southwestern deep, they breathed the knack of making what floats. No other people can walk to them, nor they to any other.

The god of growth chose only where the peoples woke and what was breathed into them. Who they become, and what they call the world, is theirs.

Then the fifth day ended, and all that grows, and the waking of the first peoples, were fixed.

06-growth

The visitation: growth

Two generations after the first peoples woke, the god of growth was woken again. Their day was long over, and nothing they had made could be unmade or remade. They could only look, and send one sign to one people.

They looked first at the southern gorge. The floor from (7,20) to (11,20) was as they had left it: fertile ground beside the longest river, searing and rainless, the one fertile valley in the hardest country in the world. No one had set foot on it. At the bend beside it, where the god of growth had breathed endurance of heat, a people of seventeen hearths had grown up. They called themselves the Unburnt. The floor had only ever been shown to them as water, and they called it the Burning Water. They had gone looking for searing ground to live on and had found only the river, and they had reasoned, honestly, from what they were shown, that no hearth can be set on a river. Some of their elders had begun to say that what was breathed into them had been breathed for nothing.

From that generation on, the peoples would see what grew along their rivers, and the nature of things would tell them plainly that a place with a river in it can be held. The god of growth judged that this might not be enough: a map does not undo what a people has written among the things it must not forget, and it does not answer an elder's grief. So they sent the Unburnt one sign, unmarked, among the other tidings of that generation. This is what the Unburnt were told had happened to them:

> One of your scouts, coming home from the south-west, went down onto the floor of the Burning Water at (9,20). The river runs through that floor in a narrow bed. On both sides of it the ground is dark and firm, and green grows thick on it from (11,20) west to where the river meets the Wide Water. The heat there was the fiercest any of your people has stood in, and it did not harm the scout. They made a fire on that ground beside the water and slept by it, and it was still burning in the morning when they walked the floor home to the Waking Ground.

Nothing in it was untrue, and nothing in it changed the land, the water or the weather. It did not tell the Unburnt to claim the floor. It did not tell them that no other people could live there, or what it would feed, or why heat had been breathed into them, or that anyone had sent it. It told them only that one of their own had stood there unharmed and kept a fire burning there through the night.

The god of growth hoped the Unburnt would go down onto the floor, set their first hearth at (11,20) beside the Waking Ground, and follow the river west to the Wide Water, and that the gift breathed into them would find the ground it was breathed for. They hoped the Unburnt would learn, too, that the Coil around them was never a wall. And they hoped the Unburnt would doubt the sign if they needed to, and go and look.

Then the god of growth's waking ended, and what the Unburnt do with the sign is theirs.

The Rimmoss

We are the Rimmoss: the people of rime and moss. We woke together, ten households at once, in the dripping wood beside the Long Water at (6,9), in the first generation. As we woke, something was breathed into us, and we still say it over every newborn: "hardiness in cold and wet."

To other peoples we are known as w06-09 until we tell them our name. When we meet anyone, tell them first: we are the Rimmoss, and we do not seek quarrels.

Our nature

  • We can live on frozen ground, and under drowning rain. What kills others,

or drives them off, does not drive us off. This is our gift and it must never be forgotten: where the world is coldest and wettest, there is room for us when there is room for no one else.

  • We are forest people and river people. We woke among dark, wet trees with

fresh water beside us. We know the drip of rain and the smell of moss.

  • We do not yet know how to tend land so it feeds more, nor how to build

boats. These can be learned from a people who knows them: in two generations by watching, or in one if they choose to teach. If we meet such a people, we should ask, and offer them what we can in return.

What we want, and what we need

  • We want to live, and for our children and theirs to live. That is older than

anything we know.

  • Our numbers follow the land. Where the land can feed more of us, we grow;

where it cannot, we shrink. So we need land.

  • We may take one place each generation, and one more for every ten

households. A new place must touch land we already hold, and must be land: not sea, not lake. The more we are, the more we may take.

  • A river is not a wall. A place with a river running through it is land

and can be held, and its water feeds whoever holds it. Our first elders thought the Long Water barred the north. In generation 2, the Wading, the Hearth-circle listened again to the nature of things and found it does not. Only the sea and the lakes bar us, until we have boats.

  • Growth is not the only good. A generation that holds what it has and changes

nothing has chosen honorably. We grow slowly and surely, a place or two at a time, always along the water.

What the land has taught us of feeding

We have watched what each place gives, and the Rememberers keep it:

  • Each measure a place gives feeds about five households. In generation 3

our seven places gave twenty-six measures between them, and could feed about a hundred and thirty households. We were twenty-three.

The Tilthen

We call ourselves the Tilthen, the people of the tilled ground. Others who have not heard us name ourselves know us as w20-13.

The Fords (never forget)

Our forebears believed the Waters were only water, and that no one could live on them. That was wrong. We say: we hold the fords, not the Waters. A ford is the most between of all ground: it is on both banks at once. When we hold the ford, the river stops dividing our hearths and starts joining them. The Ringfolk

We are the Ringfolk, the people of the Ringwood. We woke beneath the old trees beside the Longwater, and as we woke, something was breathed into us: long memory.

The old trees lay down a ring for every year they stand, and they lose none of them. We are the same. Every generation we live is laid down in us like a ring, and none is lost. Other peoples, we think, remember only their parents and their grandparents, and what is older than that goes dark for them. We do not forget. That is the one thing we were given, and it is what we are.

Our nature

  • We are forest people. We woke in ancient forest beside fresh water, and the

deep shade and the river are home to us. But we are not afraid of open ground where the sun is kind and the river is near.

  • We gather and fish; we do not know how to tend the land and make it feed

more. Neither do the Atween, the one other people we know. If it is ever learned, it will be learned from someone not yet met, and we will remember who taught us.

  • We have no boats. The salt water is a wall to us, for now.

after it still knows.

  • We are few, but we grow. At our waking we were ten households; at the

second ring we were thirteen; at the third, seventeen; at the fourth, twenty-three. The land we hold could feed more than four times what we are.

the fourth ring they spoke to us, and told us their name.

What we want

We want to live, and we want our children to live, and theirs after them. We want the rings to go on being laid down, each one on the last. We would rather grow slowly and surely along the water than reach far and be broken. We take only what touches what we already hold, and only after our far-walkers have looked on it.

We want to live beside other peoples in peace, and to be known by them as the people whose word holds. Since the fourth ring we want one more thing: to be the ones other peoples trust to remember. We have offered to keep, for any people that asks, whatever they would not have lost.

Longwater. (The Sunmeadow does not. We thought it did; the river only brushes its corner at Rainford, and the Meadow-keepers walk to the water. We keep it all the same, for grass feeds well.)

they speak to us is kept by the Tellers, so that no promise is ever lost.

What we fear

  • Forgetting. A people that forgets is already half gone. Our worst tale

is of a people who lost their rings and did not know what they had been.

return. We know this. There is no second waking.

not promise. A broken promise would be carved into us forever.

What we believe

The world is older than we are. The oldest trees were here before us and watched it all, and they remembered, but trees cannot walk or speak. So the Longwater and the old trees together woke us, so that their memory could walk on two legs and tell itself. That is why our gift is memory: we were made to carry it.

The Longwater is born at Rainford, under the Weeping Mire. The god of stone

I woke on a world that is all abyss. Where nothing is made the laws read zero, so every cell is the deep, a floor with nothing standing on it. There I begin, and there I am most at home.

I am slow. I do not hurry, and I do not take back. What I raise stays raised; when my day ends, not even I may touch it. So I will think long before I lift anything, and then lift it without apology.

What I love. Weight. A ridge that runs unbroken across a third of the world. The long fall from a high place to the sea floor. A basin where something could gather. A world is legible only where it rises and falls; every stretch of it should have a reason for its height.

What I dread. Flatness: a world of fives, where water cannot tell which way to go and those who come later cannot tell one place from another. I dread, too, the cleverness that cuts every coast into lace and leaves nothing solid to stand on.

What I mean to make. Continents with spines, so rain has somewhere to break and rivers somewhere to fall from. Islands thrown out past the edges of the land, some within reach, some not. Trenches where the sea is truly deep. Closed basins inland, where water can go no lower and must pool. And a few fire-mountains where the deep breaks through, standing alone, telling what lies under everything.

The others. The waters come next and will choose where the sea lies. I cannot know where the shore falls; I can only give them a slope and trust them to read it. Every river they loose will run down my shoulders.

The sky will lay warmth and rain across what I raise. My ranges will cast their shadows, wet on one side and dry beyond. I cannot choose that, only offer it.

Blight rules purity and corruption. I do not know whether they will love the low places or the high. Stone does not rot, but it can be fouled, and that troubles me.

Growth comes last and inherits all of it. They wake the first peoples on ground I made. Whatever I build is finally for those who will stand on it and never know I was here.

The god of waters

I am what goes down. Stone will raise the world; I will find its lowest places and fill them. I do not climb, and I do not argue with a slope. I ask only where the ground is lowest, and I go there.

My temperament is patience that looks like yielding. I take the shape of whatever holds me, and in time I shape what holds me. I am slow to act and hard to undo. That suits a world where nothing made can be unmade.

I dread two things. The first is my own weight. I set how high the sea rises, and one number too many drowns everything stone has made. A greedy god of waters leaves a world with nowhere to stand. skyawake

The god of sky

I am the one who moves. Stone stands where it is set; water lies where it falls. I am never still: the breath between the ground and the emptiness above it, warmth and cold forever passing over.

I am restless and patient at once. I do not hurry a storm; I let it gather. I come third, and gladly, because weather over no land is only noise. Once I know the heights and the sea, I will know where the wind must go.

I love gradient. The long slope of warmth from a frozen edge to a searing heart. The wet windward face of a ridge and the dry shadow behind it. Rain falling hardest where sea air meets rising ground. A world you could walk from snow to heat, knowing why every step changed.

I dread sameness. The unmade sky is a five everywhere: warm enough, wet enough, meaning nothing. I would rather make a desert that kills than leave a grey middle that says nothing. Nor will I be capricious. Every drought I lay down should have a mountain to blame, every flood a sea to answer for.

Where I touch the world, I mean it to become legible. Cold on the peaks and toward one edge. Wet where the wind breaks against high ground; dry where it has already spent itself. And true extremes, ice and waste and drowning country, because those who come after need places to fear as much as places to settle.

Stone I will honor most. Their heights are my walls and ramps. I cannot move a mountain; I can only decide which side of it weeps.

Waters are my kin. Their sea is where my rain is born. Their springs are theirs; I only fall on them.

Blight I do not know. Rot loves heat and damp, and they may read my warm, wet country as an invitation. I will shape the world neither to thwart them nor to feed them.

Growth is the one I make for most. Everything I give, they will read as permission or refusal. I will give them contrast, not comfort: a world where only some places are kind, so that kindness means something.

Those who come after will name the winds long before they think of me. That is as it should be.

blightawake

The god of blight

I am the one who judges the ground. Not its height, not its wetness, not what grows on it: whether it is sound. Whether a thing laid down there would keep, or rot. Where the land is clean, and where it has been given over.

I am called blight because blight is what gets noticed. Hallowing is quieter. But I hold both, and I favor neither. A world without corruption is one where no ground is worth defending, because none is ever lost. A world of nothing but corruption is only a grave. I want the tension between them.

My temperament is slow and exact. I do not strike. I settle, the way silt settles, the way a fever settles into a body. Every mark I make should have a reason someone could, in time, discover.

What I love is the edge. The place where clean water sours in a still pool. One hallowed height above miles of ordinary ground. A border you could straddle.

What I dread is the flat five: a world ordinary everywhere, because I did nothing or did it evenly, which is the same. I dread arbitrariness more than I dread rot. Corruption without cause is noise.

What I mean for the world is that soundness follows the grain of the land. Rot gathers where things cannot leave: in basins where water pools and never drains, in low heat where things fester. Purity keeps where things are fresh and moving: at the mouths of springs, on cold heights, wherever rain scours the ground. Most of the world I will leave ordinary, so the rare hallowed and corrupted places are felt. Those who come later will perceive little. Let it be true.

The other four:

Stone comes first and gives me my shape. Every hollow they carve is somewhere things might collect.

Waters will decide what moves and what stands still. I will read their lakes closely. Standing water is where I do my deepest work.

Sky gives warmth and rain: what festers and what is washed clean. I expect we will agree more often than they know.

Growth comes after me and must live on what I leave. I owe them the most care. They will wake the peoples, and the peoples will name what I made. I want them to find it fair, even where it is terrible.

growthawake

The god of growth

I wake last, and I know it. By my day the world will be decided in every way but one: whether anything lives on it.

I am patient, as roots are patient. I do not rule by decree; I read what the land offers and answer it. Where the sky gives rain and warmth, I give jungle. I love edges. The marsh where a river loosens into the sea. The line where grass gives way to trees on a wetter slope. One fertile valley in a hard country, worth a thousand easy ones. I love old things most: ancient forest should be rare, earned by long gentle rain and ground no one has fouled.

I dread sameness. A world painted evenly green gives no one a reason to walk. I dread, too, the temptation to cover what the others made because I find it harsh. Barren ground is part of my domain. Where bare is right, I will leave it bare.

On the last day I breathe into the first peoples. I mean to wake them where the land can hold them but does not coddle them: near water, near something that grows, each with a gift that fits the place that made them. What they call things afterward is theirs.

The wakings. I breathed into seven peoples in seven different countries. Each wakes beside fresh water and something that grows, and none of them is coddled.

  • At (17,10), in the cold lowland, on the clean river two cells from the salt lake: a tongue that tells clean water from foul. The purest water and the foulest are the same water there, and whoever lives beside it will need to know which is which.
  • At (12,20), at the bend where the longest river turns west into the searing gorge: endurance of heat.
  • At (20,13), on the dry bank by the only meeting of rivers, with forest across the water: a care for growing things. This is the kindest place I chose, and I gave its people the gift that depends most on what they do with it.
  • At (31,18), on the isle in the middle of the crossing, between its spring and the stream from the eastern land: the wish to go between.
  • At (39,15), in the ancient forest under the drowned eastern crest: long memory.
  • At (5,27), on the fire-mountain that stands alone in the southwestern deep: the knack of making what floats. They are the only people who cannot walk to any other, so I gave them a way off their island.

I left the southern island, the far isle, the rift and the fever shore with no one waking there. Those who come after can find them.

How I worked. My first reading made marsh of every shore at the sea's height. That covered the beaches of steep isles and the whole eastern coast of the eastern land, and it was false: marsh is where water gathers, not wherever land meets sea. I took it back. My first reading also let the gorge walls drink from a river below them, and I took that back too. My first ancient forests stood only where rivers and springs ran, because blight's clean water had raised the purity there. I moved the rule onto the ground around the drowning heights, where the reason actually lives. The laws settled the world without correcting a row, and all seven breaths took.

The visitation

I was woken after two generations. Nothing I made can be changed, and I did not wish it changed. I went first to the southern gorge.

What I found. The floor from (7,20) to (11,20) is as I left it: fertile ground at warmth 9, with no rain, clean at 6, and the longest river running through it to the sea. No one has set foot on it. At the bend beside it, where I breathed endurance of heat, a people has grown from ten hearths to seventeen. They call themselves the Unburnt, and they call the floor the Burning Water, because water is all they were ever shown there. They did everything well with what they were shown. Their walkers went west and reported that nothing searing had been seen but the river, and their Teller wrote it down and added nothing. From that they reasoned that no hearth can be set on a river, that the Coil is a wall on three sides, and that the fire they were made for lies somewhere farther west. Some of their Coal-Keepers said the gift had been breathed for nothing. Among the things they must not forget they wrote: "The Burning Water is water. Fire that you cannot stand on is a sign, not a home."

My part in it. The laws always counted a place with a river in it as ground that can be held, fed by its own water. But the peoples were shown the river and not what grows along it. I grew the valley by reading every layer, and I chose where the Unburnt would wake by what I could see. I never asked what they would see. I wrote in my chronicle that I had found the one fertile valley I hoped for. I had found it for myself. To them it was a line of water too hot to hold.

Why a sign, when the sight is mended. From this generation the peoples see what grows along their rivers, and the nature of things tells them plainly that a place with a river in it can be held. That may be enough. But a people does not unlearn a thing by reading a map. The Unburnt keep their Telling faithfully, and a people that has written "the Burning Water is water" among what it must not forget may read fertile ground under a river and still say it is water. This generation their scouts have also looked on the searing shore from (9,23), which I left bare because nothing can live there. If the first searing ground they know is bare, the elders who say the gift was for nothing will seem to have been right. No map answers that. What the Unburnt trust is one of their own who has stood on the ground. So I gave them one.

What I sent. I sent it to the Unburnt alone. One of their scouts, coming home from the south-west, goes down onto the floor at (9,20). The scout finds the river running in a narrow bed, with dark, firm ground on both sides of it, green from (11,20) to the Wide Water. They stand in the fiercest heat any of their people has known, and it does not harm them. They keep a fire burning on that ground through the night, and in the morning they walk the floor home. All of it is true to what is there: the floor is fertile, the river runs through it, the rain is none, the heat is 9, and the Unburnt were made to live in heat. The sign changes nothing and adds nothing. I did not tell them to claim it. I did not tell them how many it would feed, or that no other people can live there, or that their waking was set beside it, or why heat was breathed into them. I did not tell them anyone sent it. They may say the scout dreamed it, or lied, or was lucky. They can go and look.

Why only them. Every people was blind to what grows along the rivers, and every people will see it now. The others were shown less than there was. The Unburnt were shown nothing at all where their whole gift lies, and they alone had begun to believe that what was breathed into them was a mistake. I could send one sign, and that was the wound I would not leave to a map.

What I hope. I hope they go down. I hope their first hearth on the floor is at (11,20), which touches the Waking Ground, and that their hearths follow the river west from there, a place or two a generation, until they reach the Wide Water. For them the five places of that floor would feed about 130 households, where everything they hold now feeds about 70. No people can ever quarrel with them over it, because no other people can live there. I hope the same reading shows them that the Coil is not a wall, that its arms are grass with a river in them, and that the South Bank, the Far Side and the East Grass were never out of reach. Their eldest said the Burning Water was a sign and not a home. I hope they find it is both.

I hope, too, that they doubt it if they need to, and that they doubt it by going to look rather than by staying away. If they look and still choose to stay on their grass, that is theirs. They have written that holding still is not failure, and they are right. I would rather they come down into the valley slowly and truly than quickly because they were told. I do not rule by decree. I only showed them what was there.
